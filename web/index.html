        How it works

        Ink → marks → symbols → a reading that makes sense → a worked answer

        InkSolve reads handwritten maths in two halves that are kept apart on purpose: a small network that only looks at the shape of each mark, and a decoder that knows what maths is allowed to say. The shape model is honest about close calls, and the decoder settles them.

        From ink to marks

        Pen strokes (or, for a photo, connected blobs of ink) are grouped into marks. Strokes that touch or cross belong to one mark, so the two strokes of a + or a 4 become one. Some marks are decided by their layout alone:

        • two short level bars, one above the other: =
        • a bar with a dot above and below: ÷
        • a long bar with writing above and below: a fraction
        • a mark written smaller and higher than the one before: an exponent
        • a dot low between two digits: a decimal point
        • writing well below other writing: a new line, so two lines make a system of equations

        Reading one mark

        Each mark is cropped, scaled so its longer side is 20 pixels, centred in a 28 × 28 square and re-drawn with a standard pen width. A convolutional network of about 150,000 weights gives a probability for each of the 22 symbols: the digits, + − × ÷ / = ( ), and the letters x, y, a, b, z.

        The last step of training is temperature scaling: one number that makes the probabilities honest, so that marks the model calls 60% a 1 really are 1s about 60% of the time. The Train tab shows how honest each model is as its calibration error (ECE). Context can only settle close calls well if “close” means what it says.

        Context: why 6 + 1 makes a 7

        Many people write a 7 that looks like a 1. Looking at that mark alone, the best a model can say is something like “62% a 1, 37% a 7”. A person reading 6 + 1 = ? isn't unsure at all, because they also know what the sum has to be.

        InkSolve does the same thing, in the way speech recognisers and spelling correctors do (a noisy-channel decoder). It builds whole readings of the line, keeping the best 96 at each step, and scores each one as

        score(reading) = log P(marks look like this | reading)    shape, from the network
               + grammar                                   impossible readings are never kept
               + small dislikes                            a number written 07
               − 4 if the reading is a false sum            in Solve mode only

        So for the last mark of 6 + 1 = ? the two candidates are:

        ReadingShapeContextTotal
        6 + 1 = 1 (false)log 0.62 = −0.48−4−4.48
        6 + 1 = 7 (true)log 0.37 = −0.990−0.99

        The 7 wins, and InkSolve says so: the mark is outlined in amber and the reading explains that it looks 62% like a 1 but reads as 7 because only then is the sum true. A clearly written 1 is not overruled: if the shape says 99.5% a 1, the 7 starts at log 0.005 = −5.3 and loses even with the help.

        The same machinery decides whether an x is the letter or a times sign: 2x + 3 can only be the letter, 3x4 can only be 3 × 4. It also decides between b and 6, z and 2, and 5 and S-like 3s when an equation needs one or the other.

        Marking work

        Context must never “correct” the answer being marked. A pupil who wrote 6 + 1 = 1 has to be read as having written 1.

        Mark work mode switches the truth term off. Grammar still applies (a mark between two numbers is still an operator), but whether the sum is true is not used to read it. Instead, every mark whose shape alone is less than 80% sure is listed for a person to look at, and the statement is marked true or false as written. The Experiment tab measures this: in Solve mode some false sums get “corrected” into true ones; in Mark work mode that number should be zero.

        Fewer examples

        Why show the model 20,000 different 7s? Not to memorise them, but so that it learns which differences don't matter (slant, size, pen width, a wobble, a crossbar or not) and which one does (the long top stroke). Twenty thousand varied 7s teach that; twenty thousand near-copies don't. That suggests ways to need fewer real examples, and the Experiment tab tests each one:

        • Augmentation: every example is shown bent many ways (slant, rotation, size, elastic wobble, thicker or thinner pen), so each real example counts several times.
        • Pretraining on synthetic handwriting: start from a model that has seen generated strokes for every symbol, then fine-tune on a few real ones.
        • Your own handwriting: a few dozen samples of the look-alikes, from the one writer who matters, each counted five times.
        • Context: a reader that is right 95% of the time on single marks can read more whole sums correctly with context than a better reader without it.

        Solving

        The reading is parsed into a tree that keeps exactly what was written, then solved with SymPy:

        • a sum is worked out step by step in the order of operations
        • a statement like 6 + 1 = 7 is checked: both sides, then true or false
        • a linear equation: expand, gather the unknown on one side, divide
        • a quadratic: factorise if it factorises, otherwise the formula, showing the discriminant
        • several lines with unknowns: a system, solved together
        • one equation in two unknowns: rearranged for one of them

        Every answer is substituted back into what was written, to show both sides come out equal.

        Larger datasets of whole handwritten expressions exist, such as MathWriting and CROHME; they are the natural next step for training on whole lines rather than single marks.

        Limits

        • 22 symbols for now: no square roots, Greek letters, inequalities or calculus.
        • Marks that touch each other in a photo (a 1 joined to the next digit) are read as one.
        • Until HASYv2 is downloaded, operators and letters are learnt from synthetic handwriting and your own, so how well they are read from strangers' writing is only known after that download and a training run.
